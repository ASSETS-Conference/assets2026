  • Anne Spencer Ross, Bucknell University
  • Tiago Guerreiro, Universidade de Lisboa
  • Kyle Montague, Northumbria University

Abstract

The Web is shifting again: increasingly built with AI tools by people without accessibility expertise, and increasingly optimised for AI agents over human readers. As with every previous shift, this risks leaving disabled people further behind. This workshop asks what we should do about it.

Co-creating a Trauma-Informed Accessibility Research Practice

Organizers

  • Maryam Bandukda, University College London
  • Xandra Miguel-Lorenzo, University College London
  • Tigmanshu Bhatnagar, University College London
  • Victoria Austin, University College London
  • Catherine Holloway, University College London
  • Monica Lakhanpaul, University College London

Abstract

Trauma-informed care is gaining momentum across sectors that work with potentially vulnerable groups, including education, healthcare, social work, and criminal justice. In parallel, trauma-informed research and design have emerged as a growing area of interest in computing, with a marked increase in publications since 2020 addressing technology-mediated psychological trauma, technologies that support trauma survivors, and design with and for those communities.

This trend is visible across SIGACCESS, SIGCHI, and the wider ACM computing research landscape. Given the strong, bidirectional association between disability and psychological trauma, the need for shared trauma-informed practices is especially acute in accessibility research. This workshop brings together researchers, practitioners, and community members to examine what trauma-informed research looks like in our field, and to begin building shared resources and best practices.

Designing With, Not For: Inclusive Methodologies for Co-designing With People With Diverse Abilities

Organizers

  • Sophia Ppali, Delft University of Technology
  • MinYoung Yoo, Simon Fraser University
  • Laurence Myers, Delft University of Technology
  • Karthikeya Puttur Venkatraj, Centrum Wiskunde & Informatica
  • Emilia Barakova, TU Eindhoven
  • Hamed S. Alavi, University of Amsterdam
  • Judith Good, University of Amsterdam
  • Giulia Barbareschi, University of Duisburg Essen
  • Stefania Serafin, Technical University of Denmark
  • Katta Spiel, TU Wien
  • Stella Boess, Delft University of Technology
  • Alessandro Bozzon, Delft University of Technology
  • Himanshu Verma, Delft University of Technology

Abstract

Designing accessible technologies and services plays a vital role in facilitating social and civic inclusion for people with diverse abilities; yet inclusion is equally vital to creating them. The methods through which research and design are conducted should themselves be inclusive, so that people with diverse abilities can participate as co-designers and co-owners of the work. Nevertheless, researchers working across ability-diverse populations often find themselves without a clear starting point. Existing guidelines are scarce and often difficult to adapt to specific contexts and communities. Translating resources into practice requires tailored vocabularies and procedures that are rarely documented or shared. Our workshop brings together researchers and practitioners working with people with diverse abilities to begin mapping the landscape of inclusive methodological approaches. Our goal is to surface what people are already doing including adaptations, workarounds, and community-specific practices that rarely make it into publications. The outcome will be a shared resource that includes case studies and approaches that can be used as inspirational starting points that researchers can adapt to their own contexts and communities.

Safe and Accessible AI for Youth with Disabilities and Neurodivergence

Organizers

  • Yaman Yu, University of Washington
  • Diana Freed, Brown University
  • Jake Chanenson, University of Chicago
  • Jessica Vitak, University of Maryland
  • Sheena Erete, University of Maryland
  • Tamara Clegg, University of Maryland
  • Marshini Chetty, University of Chicago

Abstract

Generative AI (GenAI) systems are increasingly woven into young people's everyday lives, shaping how they learn, communicate, create, seek support, and understand themselves. For youth with disabilities and neurodivergence, these systems may offer meaningful opportunities for communication support, low-pressure social interaction, personalized learning, and self-expression.

These benefits are closely tied to new and uneven risks. AI systems may misunderstand disabled and neurodivergent users, reproduce stereotypes, encourage emotional overreliance, or rely on safeguards that are inaccessible, privacy-invasive, and overly surveillant. These concerns sit at the intersection of accessibility, youth digital safety, usable security and privacy, and AI ethics, yet they are rarely studied together. This gap matters because inaccessible or poorly designed safeguards can themselves become sources of harm.

In this half-day virtual workshop, we invite researchers, practitioners, designers, educators, advocates, and youth to examine what safe and accessible AI should mean for youth with disabilities and neurodivergence. Through shared discussion and collaborative activities, the workshop will identify key risks, design tensions, and methodological challenges, while beginning to build a shared research and design agenda for this emerging area.

Celebratory Technology for Neurodiversity: Designing for the Freedom to Be Me

Organizers

  • LouAnne Boyd, Chapman University
  • Annuska Zolyomi, University of Washington, Bothell
  • Rua Williams, Purdue University
  • Andrew Begel, Carnegie Mellon University
  • Nick Dalton, Northumbria University

Abstract

This workshop aims to address the need for technology that goes beyond accommodations for neurodivergence to celebrating neurodiversity. This is done in part, by examining how technology can intervene on normative attitudes, biases, and behaviors enacted by the general public and embedded in social practices, institutions, and infrastructure. By engaging participants in a comprehensive exploration of celebratory technology, the workshop seeks to redefine its parameters, examine existing research and examples, and collaboratively generate new ideas.

We will discuss how celebratory technology can influence various research methodologies, evaluate the impact of design choices, and address biases and stigma associated with autism and other neurodivergencies. Through participatory evaluation and brainstorming, participants will work together to develop a master plan to meet crucial needs in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) and create a framework for future research and design.
